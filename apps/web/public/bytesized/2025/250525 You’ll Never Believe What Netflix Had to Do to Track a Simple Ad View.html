Tracking an ad sounds simple. Serve the video. Log the event. Done.

But scaling that across millions of devices, dozens of vendors, and ever-changing ad formats? That takes real architecture.

Netflix launched its ad-supported plan in 2022 with a partner-managed backend. Microsoft handled serving; Netflix handled playback. Events were pushed back to vendors through a minimal feedback loop. It worked. But over time, the demands got more complex, and the gaps became clear.

So Netflix rethought the system.

🧱 Act I: The Lightweight Setup

Netflix started with a pragmatic MVP.

When a device needed an ad:

  1. The Ads Manager appended device and session metadata to the request.

  2. Microsoft returned a VAST response with tracking URLs.

  3. Netflix generated a structured protobuf carrying ad metadata.

  4. The protobuf was encrypted and handed to the client as a single opaque token.

  5. Clients sent the token back as part of playback events.

  6. A server-side Ads Event Handler decrypted it, parsed out the tracking URLs, and called vendor endpoints.

It was fast. Efficient. But brittle.

That opaque token became a crutch. It had to carry everything: ad ID, pricing info, vendor callback URLs, policy flags, impression caps, privacy bits, and more.

As Netflix added more formats and vendors (e.g., IAS, Nielsen), token size exploded. Mobile devices (already memory-constrained) started to show signs of stress. Worse, debugging became painful. Every change required tight coordination between client and server teams. The payloads weren’t just heavy; they were a liability.

📦 Act II: Decoupling with Metadata Registry

The core shift? Moving from "carry everything in the token" to "reference it server-side."

Netflix introduced a key-value metadata registry. Here’s the new flow:

  • The client sends a compact token with:

    • A hashed ad ID

    • A metadata record ID

    • The event type (e.g., start, quartile, complete)

  • The event handler retrieves full ad metadata from the registry.

  • Events are enriched server-side with tracking URLs, campaign info, and policy context.

Suddenly, the client didn’t need to know anything about downstream requirements. It just had to say what happened and when.

This unlocked faster changes, safer rollouts, and leaner device workloads. It also paved the way for a shared data contract across teams.

🧠 Act III: Centralizing with Ads Event Publisher

Decoupling was step one. Next came consolidation.

Netflix built a new central service: the Ads Event Publisher — the single ingestion point for all ad-related events. Its responsibilities include:

  • Decryption

  • Validation

  • Metadata lookups

  • Identifier hashing (for privacy)

  • Emitting normalized events to Kafka

This service is now the backbone of the ad telemetry system. It ensures that every downstream consumer (from billing to analytics to vendor relays) receives consistent, enriched, and validated data.

🔁 Think of it as a customs checkpoint: all packages get opened, inspected, labeled, and routed based on standardized rules.

⚙️ Act IV: Fanning Out

Once events hit Kafka, they fan out to a range of systems:

🧮 Metrics & Analytics

  • A Flink job processes real-time data and stores it in Druid.

  • An offline batch job reconciles discrepancies using late-arriving events or secondary sources.

🧑‍🤝‍🧑 Ad Sessionizer

  • Groups raw playback events into coherent ad sessions.

  • Enables detailed reporting like: “Was this ad completed? Skipped? Interrupted?”

🏷 Vendor Relays

  • The Ads Event Handler calls out to partners like IAS and Nielsen using stored metadata.

  • Tracking pixels and callbacks are invoked precisely, respecting vendor contracts.

💰 Billing & Revenue

  • Events are aggregated for impression counting, pricing, and revenue recognition.

  • Dynamic pricing, opt-outs, and invalidated events are reconciled offline.

📊 Reporting APIs

  • Feed dashboards for advertisers and account managers.

  • Also support compliance workflows like GDPR/DSAR exports.

📌 Lessons from the Rebuild

  1. Smaller tokens, safer updates
    Minimizing client payloads led to faster deploys and fewer breaking changes.

  2. Central publisher = better observability
    Having a single ingestion point simplified testing, debugging, and versioning.

  3. Consistent contracts boost velocity
    With clear server-side guarantees, teams could build independently and trust the data.

  4. Server-side ownership wins at scale
    Phones don’t need the full context. That’s what the backend is for: context, consistency, and control.

🔚 Final Word

The new pipeline doesn’t scream innovation. It doesn’t show up in the Netflix UI. But it underpins everything: vendor contracts, user experience, compliance, billing, even personalization.

It’s built to support display ads, shoppable video, QR overlays, and live streams. Quiet, scalable, and robust which is just how good infrastructure should be.
