The Requirements Trap

Before diving into specific mistakes, let's address why these issues occur. Teams often rush into implementation without clearly defining their system's actual requirements. Every architectural decision should map back to concrete business needs and constraints. This foundation helps avoid many of the pitfalls we'll discuss.

1. The Cache-All Antipattern

The Problem

Caching often appears deceptively simple. Just drop Redis in front of your database and watch performance soar, right? The reality is far more nuanced. Most architectures already incorporate multiple layers of caching, often in ways that aren't immediately obvious (As shown above). When these caching layers interact in unexpected ways, you can encounter serious issues such as:

  • Cache inconsistency issues

  • Memory bloat from caching unnecessary data

  • Complex invalidation logic

  • Hidden system dependencies

The Solution

Start with proper data access analysis:

  1. Profile your actual access patterns

  2. Identify hot spots in your data

  3. Implement cache warming strategies

  4. Define clear TTL policies based on business requirements

  5. Document cache invalidation strategies

Caches can provide faster responses, happier users, smoother systems. But under the surface, they could end up being a minefield for potential issues. If you’re thinking about introducing a cache, pause and consider:

  1. How will you handle cache invalidation? Stale data can lead to unpredictable behavior. What’s your strategy for ensuring your cache stays fresh and reliable?

  2. Can you see what’s in your cache? Is it a black box where bugs hide, or a white box you can inspect and debug? Visibility is the difference between quick fixes and all-night troubleshooting.

  3. What happens during a cold start? An empty cache means every request hits the backend. Can your system survive that load? Or are you building a single point of failure?

  4. What’s the real cost of your cache? Every cache adds complexity. Do the performance gains outweigh the risks of misses, overhead, and debugging challenges?

Great system design is about trade-offs. A cache might be the right move, but only if you’ve answered these questions first.

2. The Microservice Maze

The Problem

Microservices have become the default architecture choice, often without proper consideration of the overhead they introduce. We talk about this in far more detail in our previous post Why Microservices Aren’t Always the Right Answer. Without the right checks and balances in place systems can end up with:

  • Excessive network calls

  • Complex deployment pipelines

  • Difficult debugging scenarios

  • Increased operational complexity

The Solution

Design services around business capabilities:

  • Start with a monolith for new products

  • Consider a Modular Monolith as a first step for scaling

  • When needed, split services considering both team boundaries and business domains

  • Use the "2 Pizza Team" rule - if a service requires more than 2 teams to maintain, it's likely too big

  • Implement proper service discovery and circuit breakers

Amazon's organizational structure exemplifies this approach - teams are organized around business capabilities, with services matching these boundaries.

3. Database Separation Anxiety

The Problem

One of the most pervasive mistakes in distributed systems is treating databases like a shared drive. Teams build "microservices" that are really just thin layers around a shared database. This creates a hidden monolith, one that's harder to maintain than an actual monolithic application.

Key issues include:

  • Schema changes affect multiple services simultaneously

  • Services become tightly coupled through shared data models

  • Scaling becomes a nightmare as different services have different data access patterns

  • Transaction boundaries become unclear

The Solution

Keep in mind proper domain-driven design. Each service should:

  • Own its data exclusively

  • Have its own dedicated storage

  • Define clear contracts for data access

  • Use event-driven patterns for data synchronization

When Uber moved from a monolithic to a microservice architecture, they implemented Domain-Oriented Microservice Architecture (DOMA). Each service owned its data, with clear boundaries and interfaces. This enabled teams to deploy independently and scale different parts of the system based on specific needs.

4. Async Gone Wrong

The Problem

Message queues and event-driven architectures are often implemented as a silver bullet for decoupling services. However, this introduces significant hidden complexity that can destabilize your entire system:

  • Message ordering becomes critical yet nearly impossible to guarantee across distributed systems

  • Dead letter queues fill up silently until a cascade of failures occurs

  • Race conditions emerge in seemingly unrelated services due to parallel processing

  • Retry storms can overwhelm downstream services during partial outages

  • Message duplicates create data inconsistencies that are incredibly difficult to debug

  • Business transactions span multiple queues with no clear rollback mechanism

  • System-wide backpressure becomes nearly impossible to implement effectively

The Solution

Design your async architecture with these battle-tested patterns:

  1. Message Flow Design

  • Map out the complete lifecycle of every message type

  • Identify all possible failure modes at each step

  • Create explicit contracts for message structure and handling

  • Implement correlation IDs that trace entire business transactions

  • Design for idempotency from the ground up

  1. Reliability Patterns

  • Implement the Outbox Pattern for guaranteed message delivery

  • Use the Saga Pattern for distributed transactions

  • Deploy Circuit Breakers with adaptive timeouts

  • Implement exponential backoff with jitter for retries

  • Create separate queues for different priority levels

  • Set up Dead Letter Queues with automated alerting and replay capabilities

  1. Operational Excellence

  • Implement message tracing across your entire system

  • Create dashboards showing queue depths and processing rates

  • Set up alerting for abnormal message flow patterns

  • Build tools for message replay and queue management

  • Maintain a message catalog documenting all event types

For example, Netflix's Stream Processing Architecture Netflix processes billions of events daily using Apache Flink for real-time analytics. They implement:

  • Exactly-once processing guarantees

  • Automatic scaling based on queue depth

  • Sophisticated replay mechanisms for data corrections

  • End-to-end monitoring of message flow

  • Automated failure recovery

5. The Monitoring Afterthought

The Problem

Modern distributed systems can fail in ways that are impossible to predict during development. Without proper observability:

  • Problems are discovered by customers instead of monitoring

  • Engineers spend hours or days piecing together what happened

  • Root cause analysis becomes guesswork

  • System behavioral changes go unnoticed until they become critical

  • Performance degradations creep in gradually

  • Capacity planning is based on gut feel rather than data

The Solution

Implement a comprehensive observability strategy across these key pillars:

  1. Metrics

  • Define and track the Four Golden Signals: Latency, Traffic, Errors, Saturation

  • Implement RED metrics (Rate, Errors, Duration) for all services

  • Set up USE metrics (Utilization, Saturation, Errors) for all resources

  • Create business metrics tied to customer experience

  • Build capacity planning dashboards with predictive alerts

  1. Logging

  • Implement structured logging with consistent fields across services

  • Create log severity guidelines and enforce them

  • Set up log aggregation with efficient search capabilities

  • Maintain audit logs for all important business events

  • Implement log sampling for high-volume events

  1. Tracing

  • Deploy distributed tracing across all services

  • Add business context to traces for easier debugging

  • Create service dependency maps from trace data

  • Track critical user journeys end-to-end

  • Implement trace sampling strategies that capture important events

  1. Alerting

  • Create alert runbooks for common scenarios

  • Implement alerting based on Service Level Objectives (SLOs)

  • Set up staged alerting with different severity levels

  • Build automated remediation for common issues

  • Maintain an alert catalog with clear ownership

🌟 Why This Matters

These architectural decisions compound over time. What starts as a minor design choice becomes a major roadblock as systems scale. Getting these fundamentals right early saves months of refactoring later.

🔄 Key Takeaways for Senior Engineers

  1. Start with clear boundaries and simple designs

  2. Make conscious decisions about trade-offs

  3. Plan for operability from day one

  4. Design for failure at every level

  5. Invest in observability early

🧠 Extra Considerations

The real art in system design isn't avoiding trade-offs, it's making them consciously and documenting the reasoning. Modern systems require balancing competing concerns:

  • Consistency vs. availability

  • Simplicity vs. flexibility

  • Performance vs. maintainability

  • Cost vs. reliability

Document your architectural decisions, including:

  • Why certain choices were made

  • What alternatives were considered

  • Known limitations and trade-offs

  • Future expansion points

The best system designs aren't necessarily the most clever or complex. They're the ones that solve business problems reliably and can be maintained by future teams. Keep it simple, document your decisions, and focus on operational excellence from day one.
