Top Anti-Patterns to Avoid in Service Architecture

A service architecture can end up slower to change, harder to operate, and less reliable than the single large system it replaced, and it can cost more to run while doing it. This is rarely the work of a careless team.

How many decisions does it take to reach that point?

It rarely takes a single bad one. The path to such a situation is built from individually sound choices, a clean separation here, an independent deployment there, a new service each time a part of the system felt distinct enough to stand on its own. Those reasonable steps accumulate into an arrangement no one would have chosen on purpose, and the problems that emerge look like a catalog of separate mistakes even though nearly all of them trace back to one early decision about how to break a system.

At a basic level, a service is a part of a system that can be deployed on its own and controls its own data. This means it does not reach into any other service’s database to do its work. It also talks to other services over a network. Inside a single program, one function calling another takes a few nanoseconds and either returns an answer or raises an error. The same call across a service boundary can take a few milliseconds, and it can also time out or succeed halfway and leave things in an odd state. Almost every anti-pattern below emerges from this one problem.

In this article, we will look at some of the most important anti-patterns in service architecture, how they happen, and how they can be avoided.

Splitting Early

The worst moment to commit to where a system divides is the very beginning, because that is when the least is known about where the divisions truly belong. Premature decomposition means breaking a system into separate services before the work has revealed which parts belong together, or before traffic or team size justifies the cost of running them separately.

It usually arrives with good intentions. The team starts fresh, wants to build the system properly, and goes for separate services on day one because that feels like the mature choice. The trouble then shows itself slowly. There are soon more services than teams to own them, a small change needs edits across three of them, and a coordinated release

Two major problems follow:

  • The boundaries get drawn with almost no information about where they belong, so they are likely placed incorrectly, and correcting them later is not a quick refactor.

  • Moving responsibility from one service to another means moving its data, its callers, and its deployment.

Wrong Boundaries

This is the mistake that matters most, because a boundary drawn in the wrong place forces nearly every problem that follows.

A wrong boundary usually comes from dividing the system along the lines easiest to see rather than the ones that matter. The easy lines are technical, a service for the user interface, a service for the data layer, a service for each database table. The line that matters is the business capability, the full set of behavior and data that changes together for one real reason.

The easy lines are chosen because technical layers and tables are visible right now, while business capabilities require understanding the domain, which takes time.

The clearest sign of a wrong boundary is that most features cross several services. Adding a field to checkout touches the order service, the pricing service, and the inventory service, none of which can do anything useful without calling the others.

When behavior that belongs together is split apart, the services constantly talk to each other, chain calls, and reach into the same data. Ideally, things that change together should live together.

Drawing those lines well is not only a technical question, which is where a 1967 observation becomes useful.

Conway’s Law

A system tends to copy the communication structure of the organization that builds it, which means boundaries are partly a question about people, not only about code. Melvin Conway noticed this in 1967. To put it plainly, if four teams build a system, that system tends to end up with four major parts that match how those teams talk to each other.

It goes wrong when the architecture is drawn on a whiteboard with no thought for who will own each piece. At some point, a boundary ends up running through the middle of two teams, and the service on that line belongs to both of them and fully to neither. The everyday symptom is that almost every change to that service needs a meeting between teams, with nobody able to say clearly who owns it.

What it costs is the exact thing splitting was supposed to deliver. Separate services promise that each team can move on its own. On the other hand, boundary across teams replaces that independence with coordination, which is slower than the single codebase the team started with.

Chatty Services

Constant back-and-forth between services usually means a boundary is in the wrong place, not that there is a performance problem to manage. Chatty services have to exchange a high volume of small calls to get anything done, because the information one of them needs is managed by another.

This follows directly from a wrong boundary.

When related data is split across services, a service spends its time asking others for pieces it would have had on hand in a single system. A common version is fetching a list and then making a network call for every item in it, turning one logical request into dozens of real ones.

It is easy to spot in a trace, where rendering a single page sets off a flurry of calls between services, and most of the time goes to waiting.

The damage compounds in two directions, since every call adds network time and every call is one more thing that can fail. Caching and batching can hide some of this, but they treat the symptom. The call volume is high because the division between services is wrong.

Call Chains

Stacking synchronous calls into a chain ties the speed and reliability of the whole request to the sum of its parts, and the maths works against the system.

A call chain is what happens when service A waits on service B, which waits on service C, which waits on service D, each one blocked until the next answers. It becomes the default because a blocking call is the simplest thing to reason about, so a service asks another and waits. The pattern repeats down the line.

The result is a single request that expands into a deep tree of calls, with a worst-case response time far higher than expected.

Two problems follow:

  • Response time adds up along the chain, since every hop adds delay.

  • Reliability does something worse. If five services in a chain are each available 99.9 percent of the time, the chain as a whole is available only about 99.5 percent of the time, because every link has to work for the request to succeed.

The result is a system less reliable than any single service inside it.

Shared Database

When two services read and write the same database, the boundary between them is meaningless, because shared data means shared fate. On paper, there are separate services, but underneath, they are bound together by the one thing that was never actually divided.

It happens because splitting code is straightforward while splitting data is hard, so teams divide the services and leave the single database in place. They may want to separate it later, which rarely happens. A wrong boundary makes it worse, since data that should have stayed together is now needed by several services, and the easiest way to give them access is to let them all touch the same tables.

It becomes obvious during a schema change, when altering one service’s data model forces a coordinated migration across several others that depend on the same structure.

What gets lost is the independence that justified the split. A service can no longer change its data freely, deployments move together, and a single slow query from one service can drag down every other service sharing that database. A service that owns its data keeps it private and lets others reach it through an interface, not the tables directly.

Saga

A saga is the price a system pays for splitting data that a single database transaction used to protect, and it becomes unmanageable when it becomes difficult to see the full story in one place. In a single database, an operation like placing an order either fully happens or fully does not. Once the order, the payment, and the inventory live in separate services, that guarantee is gone, and a saga is the pattern that tries to recreate it.

A saga runs the operation as a series of steps, each in its own service, and gives each step a matching action that undoes it if a later step fails. For example, there could be steps like reserving the stock, charging the card, and if shipping cannot be arranged, refund the card and release the stock.

The pattern can go wrong when no single place describes the whole operation, and the undo logic has grown as large and intricate as the main path. The price is steep. Other services see the intermediate states before they are rolled back. The system gives up immediate consistency, and failure handling becomes the most complicated part of the code.

Typically, a saga can be coordinated by a central controller or by services reacting to each other’s events. However, both can become harder to live with than the transaction they replaced if not properly managed.

Distributed Monolith

A distributed monolith is a collection of services so tightly coupled that they have to be deployed together, changed together, and fail together. The separate parts exist, but none of them can actually move on its own.

Nobody sets out to build a distributed monolith. It accumulates as wrong boundaries create the chatter and the call chains, a shared database binds the services together, and the team ends up running many services that behave like one program scattered across a network.

Release time makes it plain that shipping one service means shipping several others at once, and a change in one ripples outward into the rest.

The result is the worst combination available. The system pays the full price of distribution, the network calls, the operational overhead, and the cross-process debugging, while keeping the rigidity of a single program. It is harder to repair than an ordinary monolith, because the coupling now spreads across the network, where it is far less visible.

Observability

Without a way to follow a request as it moves across services, every problem above stays invisible until it becomes expensive. That makes observability a fundamental requirement for a service architecture. It means seeing what the system is doing through its logs, its metrics, and the ability to trace a single request across every service it touches.

The gap appears because the split happens first, and the visibility comes later. In a single program, a problem can be traced with a stack trace and a debugger, since the whole request lives in one place. However, once it travels across many services, those familiar tools no longer follow it.

Day to day, it appears as an incident that sends the team into five separate dashboards, still unable to answer a simple question about what happened to one request. Worse, this multiplies every other problem on the list. It is not caused by a wrong boundary, but it is the reason the slow drift toward a distributed monolith goes unseen.

So what does this suggest in practice?

The important move is to earn the right to split rather than assume it. When one of these shows up, the high call volume, the fragile chain, the shared table, the sprawling saga, the first question is whether the boundary underneath it is in the right place. Redrawing a boundary is harder than adding another workaround, but it is usually the change that helps.

Summary

In this article, we’ve looked at the most important anti-patterns when dealing with a service-based architecture. Here are the key learning points in brief:

  • Most service architecture problems are not separate mistakes to memorize but the same underlying issue, a boundary drawn too early or in the wrong place, in different forms.

  • Splitting early commits the team to boundaries when it understands the system least and can change them only at the highest cost.

  • The wrong boundary matters most, and the rule for getting it right is that things that change together should stay together.

  • A system mirrors how the teams that build it communicate, so a boundary across teams trades away the independence that splitting was meant to provide.

  • Frequent back-and-forth calls between services point to a misplaced boundary rather than a performance problem, and caching only hides it.

  • Stacking blocking calls into a chain makes response time add up and reliability fall, so the whole can be less reliable than any single service within it.

  • A shared database quietly couples services and removes their independence, which is why a service should keep its data private and expose it through an interface.

  • A saga is the cost of splitting data that one transaction used to protect, and an elaborate one often means the boundary should not have been crossed.

  • A distributed monolith is the sum of these problems, with the full cost of distribution and the rigidity of a single program, and it is worse than what it replaced.

  • Tracing a request across services is the cost of admission, and the discipline is to earn the right to split and treat every symptom as a question about the boundary beneath it.
