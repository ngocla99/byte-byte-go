A Detailed Guide to Idempotency, Delivery Semantics, and Deduplication

What happens when a service sends a request to charge a customer, but the request times out with no response? The burning question is whether the charge went through. Or should it be retried?

Two different things could have happened. The charge succeeded, and the confirmation was lost on the way back, or the request never reached the payment service at all. Both possibilities produce identical outputs, which makes it difficult to figure out what happened and the next action to be taken. Retrying risks charging the customer twice. On the other hand, declining to retry risks never charging them at all.

Idempotency is the property that makes the retry safe. An operation is idempotent when applying it more than once produces the same state as applying it once. For example, setting an account balance to 500 is idempotent, because even the tenth time to execute this operation, the outcome will be the same. In contrast, adding 500 to a balance is not idempotent, since every time it is executed, the balance amount changes. Most operations that matter in a business system resemble the second one.

In this article, we will look at the following topics in detail:

  • The three different delivery semantics available for developers.

  • The three points where duplicates enter a producer, broker, and consumer path, and why a fix at one point does nothing for the other two

  • The difference between an operation that is idempotent by nature and an endpoint engineered to behave that way

  • What does an idempotency key need to work, and how can it fail?

  • Why every deduplication scheme has a time limit, and what the guarantee is worth once that limit passes

  • What “exactly-once” means in real-world systems, and where each guarantee ends?

Delivery Semantics

In a typical event-driven setup, a sender transmits a message and needs confirmation that it arrived, so the receiver returns an acknowledgment. That acknowledgment travels the same unreliable network and can also be lost, which leaves the receiver without confirmation that the sender got the message or not. Adding an acknowledgment for the acknowledgment moves the same uncertainty one step further along the chain without eliminating it. However long the chain grows, the last message in it remains unconfirmed. This is a classic result in distributed systems, usually taught as the Two Generals Problem.

See the diagram below:

This leaves two main options:

  • At-most-once: In this approach, the sender does not retry. Duplicate sends never occur, but messages can be lost whenever a request or its acknowledgment goes missing.

  • At-least-once: In this approach, the sender retries until an acknowledgment arrives. Nothing is lost, but duplicates can occur whenever the original request succeeded, but its acknowledgment did not.

A third option is to support “exactly-once” delivery, which would require the sender to distinguish a lost request from a lost acknowledgment, but we will come to that later.

The most common practical response is to stop trying to control how many times a message arrives and start controlling the impact of duplicate messages on the actual data. A receiver that identifies a repeated message and discards it turns at-least-once delivery into an outcome where the effect occurs only once. The common term for this is “effectively once”. We can think of it as exactly-once processing instead of exactly-once delivery.

To be clear, “effectively once” is not a third delivery mode sitting alongside the other two. It is “at-least-once” delivery plus additional work at the receiver’s end. The duplicate messages are still generated, but are discarded before they can cause unintended consequences.

Duplicate Sources

Let us now understand how these duplicate messages appear in the first place.

Consider a common setup where a producer publishes messages to a broker, a consumer reads from the broker, and the consumer then does something in the outside world, such as writing to a database or sending an email. Duplicates can enter a message path at three separate points in this common arrangement:

  • Producer to broker: The producer publishes a message. The broker appends it and returns an acknowledgment, which is lost for some reason. The producer retries, and the broker appends a second copy. Two records now exist for one logical event.

  • Broker to consumer: The broker delivers a message. The consumer processes it and then crashes before acknowledging. No record of completion exists on the broker side, so the message is redelivered by the broker.

  • Consumer to external system: The consumer processes the same message twice for either of the reasons above, and each pass sends an email. This segment is entirely outside the broker’s visibility.

This arrangement is not specific to any single product, but is present in the very foundation of event-driven architecture.

Each origin has a different cause and a different owner, which means a fix at one point does nothing for the other two. Enabling producer-side deduplication removes the first category and leaves the duplicate email or processing problem entirely untouched.

This is where the advice to “just make it idempotent” starts to lose impact in a design review. Where exactly should things be idempotent? Should it be at the producer’s publishing, at the consumer’s processing, or at the email?

Operations and Endpoints

Some operations have the idempotent property built into what they do. However, most do not.

An operation is naturally idempotent when it specifies a final state rather than a change. For example, “SET balance = 500” sets the exact value the record should hold. Running it once, twice, or ten times produces the same row. In other words, the operation carries its own answer.

In contrast, ADD 500 specifies a change instead. The first application produces 500, the second produces 1000, and the tenth produces 5000. The same applies to appending a row, charging a card, decrementing inventory, and sending a notification. These operations have no natural defense against repetition, so any such defense has to be created within the design.

This distinction can become a little confusing due to how the HTTP method semantics are usually summarized. The common idea is that GET, PUT, and DELETE are idempotent while POST is not, which implies something more specific.

RFC 9110 defines a request method as idempotent when the intended effect on the server of several identical requests matches the effect of a single one [2]. To summarize, the specification defines idempotency only in terms of the effect the client intends. However, honoring that intent falls to the server. Nothing in the specification prevents a server from exposing a non-idempotent handler behind an idempotent method [3].

In other words, the method describes an obligation rather than a guarantee. A DELETE handler is idempotent only if it was written to be idempotent. A DELETE handler that decrements a counter on every call is not idempotent.

Two further points to keep in mind from the same specification are as follows:

  • A request method can be idempotent while still producing side effects the client did not request, such as log entries or revision history.

  • Response codes need not match across repeats. A first DELETE commonly returns 200 and a second returns 404, and both are consistent with an idempotent handler [3].

This leads to the practical question.

What does it take to construct the idempotent property for an operation that lacks it?

Idempotency Keys

Constructing idempotency for an operation that lacks it requires three components working together. The three are a stable identity, a durable record of what has already been done, and an atomic claim on that record.

Let’s look at the components in more detail:

  • Identity: The client generates a key and sends it with the request. The key must identify the logical operation rather than the individual attempt. Generating a fresh key within the retry loop produces code that appears correct, but provides no protection because every attempt seems like a new operation. A well-formed key identifies something like “charge order 4471,” which stays constant, however many attempts it takes.

  • The Durable Record: The server stores the key alongside the status and result of the first request. Stripe’s API documentation describes this behavior directly. The status code and body of the first request under a given key are saved and returned for subsequent requests carrying the same key, including 500 responses [4]. A retry receives the original outcome.

  • The Atomic Claim: This part is a little tricky. The obvious implementation reads the key store, finds nothing, performs the work, and writes the key. However, two concurrent requests carrying the same key both complete the read before either completes the write, so both find nothing, and both perform the work. The check and the action are separate operations, and the duplicate appears in the interval between them.

In one of their articles, Stripe talks about a second constraint that is easy to overlook. Incoming parameters are compared against those of the original request, and a mismatch produces an error rather than a replay [4]. Without that comparison, one key could authorize an operation quite different from the one it originally covered.

One limit still remains after all of this. A process can claim the key, begin the charge, and crash before recording the outcome, which leaves a record marked in progress and no local information about whether the money moved. While it is possible to narrow that interval, closing it entirely is not.

Deduplication Windows

The durable record we talked about in the previous section is not meant to last forever. This is because storing every key permanently means unbounded growth in a data store that sits directly on the request path. Therefore, every implementation prunes old entries.

A duplicate arriving while the record still exists in the data store is discarded. The same duplicate arriving after the record has been removed is indistinguishable from new work, and the operation runs again in full.

Some systems that provide the deduplication feature talk about their retention periods. The numbers can vary considerably. For example:

  • Amazon SQS FIFO queues track a message deduplication ID within a five-minute deduplication interval by default [5].

  • Stripe allows keys to be removed once they are at least 24 hours old, and states that a key reused after the original has been pruned generates a new request [4].

  • Apache Kafka expires an idempotent producer’s ID after one day by default. KIP-854, the proposal that introduced a separate configuration for this, records that when a producer ID expires, the producer “loses its idempotency guarantees” [6].

The practical takeaway is to compare the retention window against the longest realistic retry path in the system, including the paths that are easy to forget. Some examples are as follows:

  • A batch job that fails overnight and reruns the next morning.

  • A message lying in a dead letter queue and replayed by hand days later.

  • A client with exponential backoff whose last attempt takes place hours after its first.

Each of those can exceed a five-minute window comfortably, and several can exceed a day. A retry arriving after the window has closed is new work by definition rather than by malfunction.

Exactly-Once Semantics

What does exactly-once mean in the products that offer it?

To put it simply, every exactly-once guarantee in a real product covers a specific segment and is well-documented.

Let’s take the example of Apache Kafka.

A Kafka producer runs with idempotence enabled, which is the default when no conflicting configuration is set [7]. It publishes a message, receives no acknowledgment, and the client library retries internally. The duplicate is discarded at the broker.

Now let us change one detail. The application code catches the error and calls send a second time on its own.

How many messages reach the log?

The answer is two. Kafka assigns each producer an ID, and each batch a sequence number per partition. A batch whose sequence number has already been appended is discarded rather than written again [8]. The library’s internal retry carries the original sequence number and is identified as a repeat.

However, a fresh call from application code produces a new record with a new sequence number, which is not a repeat by any measure available at the broker. Kafka’s API documentation is explicit here, advising that applications avoid re-sends at their own level precisely because those cannot be deduplicated [9].

The same documentation is equally precise about the outer boundary, noting that idempotence holds only for messages sent within a single producer session [9], and KIP-854 scopes the guarantee to a single partition within that session [6]. In other words, cross a partition boundary or restart the process, and the conditions of the guarantee have changed.

Taking another example, Google Cloud draws its line in a different place because Pub/Sub addresses a different segment. Its exact-once delivery applies within a single cloud region and only to pull subscriptions, with push and export subscriptions excluded [10]. Google’s announcement of the feature also includes a section on side effects, stating that they fall strictly outside its scope on the grounds that Pub/Sub has neither connectivity nor control over whatever system produces them [1].

Looking at these two examples provides us with three specific questions we can ask about any “exactly-once” claim:

  • Exactly-once for what? Is it delivery, processing, or the effect on an external system?

  • Between which two points? Every guarantee covers a specific segment.

  • Within what window? Every deduplication scheme has a retention period.

Summary

In this article, we’ve looked at idempotency in detail. Here are the key learning points in brief:

  • A timed-out request is ambiguous. The work may have completed or may never have started, and nothing available to the caller distinguishes those cases.

  • No finite exchange of acknowledgments resolves that ambiguity, because the last message in any chain is always unconfirmed.

  • Every system therefore chooses between at-most-once, which risks loss, and at-least-once, which risks duplicates. No third option exists at the network layer.

  • “Effectively-once” is “at-least-once” delivery plus a receiver that discards repeats. The work moves to the receiving end rather than disappearing.

  • Duplicates enter at three independent points: producer retries, broker redelivery, and consumer reprocessing after a crash. A fix at one point does nothing for the other two.

  • An operation is naturally idempotent when it sets a final state. Most business operations increment, append, or notify, and are not.

  • HTTP method semantics describe intended effects rather than guaranteed ones. A handler behind PUT or DELETE is idempotent only if it was written that way.

  • An idempotency key protects nothing alone. It needs a durable record of what was already done and an atomic claim on that record.

  • The key must identify the logical operation rather than the individual attempt. A fresh key per retry reads correctly and protects nothing.

  • Every deduplication scheme has a retention window, and past it, the same request becomes new work. Documented windows run from five minutes to a full day.

  • Every exactly-once guarantee covers a named segment. The segment between a consumer and its external side effects belongs to the application.
